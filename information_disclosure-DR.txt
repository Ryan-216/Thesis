### 2.3 Information Disclosure

Information disclosure refers to the act of an organization  communicating information to external stakeholders, serving as a  fundamental mechanism to mitigate information asymmetry (Akerlof 1970).  In traditional principal-agent frameworks, asymmetry can lead to adverse selection and moral hazard. To reduce these frictions,  organizations—ranging from public corporations to non-profit  entities—often engage in voluntary disclosure. In the context of  open-source communities and cryptocurrency ecosystems, where formal  hierarchical contracts are often absent, information disclosure assumes a critical role in coordinating community action and incentivizing  contributors.

**2.3.1 Voluntary Disclosure and Signaling**
 Drawing on Signaling Theory, high-quality organizations possess a strong incentive to voluntarily disclose information to distinguish themselves from lower-quality counterparts (Spence 1973). When disclosure is  discretionary rather than mandated, it functions as a credible signal of organizational attributes. Empirical literature demonstrates that  voluntary disclosure can reduce the cost of capital and enhance external trust (Healy and Palepu 2001).
 In the non-profit and crowdfunding sectors, the disclosure of financial  flows and donation allocation is particularly potent. Saxton and Guo  (2011) find that web-based disclosure significantly boosts donor  confidence and subsequent contributions by signaling accountability. For the Bitcoin community, donation announcements by funding organizations  (e.g., Brink) effectively serve as voluntary signals. By disclosing  donation activities, these organizations signal resource availability  and a commitment to sustaining the ecosystem (Connelly et al. 2011).  However, the effectiveness of a signal is contingent upon its  observability and fit; different types of signals—such as general  organizational updates versus specific grantee details—may yield  different interpretations among receivers (Taeuscher 2019).

**2.3.2 Information Transparency as Governance**
 In decentralized open-source software (OSS) communities, information  transparency transcends mere signaling; it functions as a soft  governance mechanism. Lacking formal administrative authority, OSS  projects rely on transparent decision-making and information sharing to  establish legitimacy and norms (O'Mahony and Ferraro 2007).
 Transparency facilitates collaboration by reducing uncertainty regarding project sustainability and fairness. Research in digital platforms  suggests that detailed information disclosure generally increases  support by reducing perceived risk (Goswami et al. 2018). Nevertheless,  transparency is not universally beneficial; it acts as a "double-edged  sword" (Bernstein 2012). The strategic choice of disclosure  granularity—what Granados et al. (2010) term "transparent strategy"—can  alter stakeholder behavior. Specifically, while broad transparency  (untargeted disclosure) may foster general trust, highly granular  transparency regarding specific rewards (targeted disclosure) might  trigger social comparison or privacy concerns, potentially crowding out  intrinsic motivation or altering the perceived nature of the community  interaction (Frey and Jegen 2001). This theoretical tension highlights  the necessity of distinguishing between the impacts of targeted and  untargeted donation disclosures in open-source development.
